Ah, Greek mythology. The source of half our high school reading lists and 100% of our appreciation for petty drama. Today, we're looking at Odysseus—or Ulysses, if you prefer the Latin version or just really like James Joyce. He is the main character of the Odyssey and basically the guy who carried the Greeks on his back during the Trojan War, even though he absolutely did not want to be there in the first place.
To understand Odysseus, you have to look at his family tree. On one side, he’s a descendant of Zeus, because it’s a Greek myth and everyone is related to Zeus. But on his mother’s side, his grandfather was Autolycus, whose actual nickname was "Lone Wolf" and who was famous for being a master thief and trickster. This gives Odysseus a weird sort of dual identity: he’s half legendary epic hero and half slippery folklore con man. In fact, his grandfather named him Odysseus because it roughly translates to "man of pain" or "man of odium," depending on whether you're looking at the Greek or Latin roots. It’s highly appropriate because throughout his life, Odysseus spends an equal amount of time causing severe pain to others and suffering terrible pain himself. Later writers, like Sophocles in his play Ajax, even started a rumor that Odysseus was actually the son of Sisyphus—the guy famous for cheating death and rolling a boulder up a hill for eternity. Calling him Sisyphus's son was basically the ancient Greek version of a diss track, implying he wasn't just a rogue, but actively sinister.
Unlike Achilles, who had a super hardcore childhood being dipped in the River Styx and raised by a centaur, Odysseus had a surprisingly wholesome upbringing. We know his housekeeper, Eurycleia, was his nurse. We know his dad, Laertes, took him on walks through the orchard to teach him the names of trees. And we know he got a permanent scar on his leg while hunting a wild boar, which basically functions as a barcode for his identity later in life.
When the Trojan War kicks off, the Greek leaders show up in Ithaca to recruit him. Odysseus, fully aware that a war across the sea sounds like a terrible time, decides to fake insanity. His plan is to yoke a horse and an ox together—which makes no sense for plowing—and start sowing his fields with sea salt instead of seeds. It was a solid attempt, but a rival smart guy named Palamedes sees right through it. Palamedes takes Odysseus's infant son, Telemachus, and throws him right in front of the oncoming plow. Odysseus is forced to swerve to avoid flattening his own baby, immediately exposing that he is perfectly sane and just a massive slacker.
Despite trying to dodge the draft, once Odysseus gets to Troy, he turns out to be the ultimate group project partner. In Homer’s Iliad, he completely dominates the political assembly, leads high-stakes expeditions, and gets picked to head the super important embassy to go beg Achilles to start fighting again. At one point, a warrior named Diomedes chooses Odysseus as his partner for a nighttime stealth mission. They sneak out, catch a Trojan spy named Dolon, interrogate him, and then proceed to slaughter a sleeping Thracian king named Rhesus before stealing his legendary horses. Odysseus is just as effective during the day. He routes entire lines of Trojans even while wounded. He even dominates the post-battle entertainment; at Patroclus's funeral games, he wins the footrace and goes toe-to-toe with Ajax—who is twice his size—felling him twice in a wrestling match until Achilles has to step in and call it a draw.
Now, the Iliad actually ends before the war does, but other epic poems that only survive in fragments filled in the rest. After Achilles gets killed, Odysseus and Ajax team up to rescue his body from the battlefield. Achilles's mom, Thetis, decides to award her son's legendary armor to whichever Greek warrior did the most work. It comes down to Odysseus and Ajax. Odysseus wins the debate, and Ajax is so profoundly humiliated by the loss that he literally commits suicide. Even when Odysseus goes to the Underworld later in the Odyssey and tries to apologize with some nice, conciliatory words, Ajax’s ghost is still so salty that he literally just walks away without speaking to him.
After the armor incident, Odysseus spends his time securing the win for the Greeks through a mix of genius and horrific war crimes. He is the literal architect of the Trojan Horse, which finally breaks the ten-year siege. He also teams up with Diomedes again for a couple of side quests. First, they sail to the island of Lemnos to retrieve Philoctetes, a warrior they had abandoned years earlier, because a prophecy said they needed his magical bow to win. Then, they sneak into Troy and steal the Palladium, a sacred statue of Athena that was actively projecting a magical defense shield over the city.
But his track record isn't totally clean. During the sack of Troy, Odysseus hurls Hector’s infant son, Astyanax, off a high tower to ensure the royal bloodline ends. He also gets into a physical fistfight with his best friend Diomedes over who gets credit for stealing the statue. And finally, he never forgave Palamedes for ruining his fake-insanity plow routine. Odysseus and Diomedes frame Palamedes for treason, leading to his execution. This sparked a whole genre of ancient legal speeches, like Gorgias’s Defence of Palamedes and Alcidamas’s Against the Treachery of Palamedes, proving that even thousands of years ago, people love a good true-crime conspiracy.
So, that’s Odysseus. A loving family man who just wanted to farm his salt, but ended up becoming a stealth-assassin, a master debater, a thief of sacred relics, and the ultimate reason Troy is currently a pile of ashes.
